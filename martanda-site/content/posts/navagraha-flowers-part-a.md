*A brief research note — Part A. Continued in [Part B: Flowers of the Gods](/posts/flowers-of-the-gods-part-b/).*

The "secret" behind the Navagraha flowers is not a lost manuscript. It is a single rule, hidden in plain sight in the Yājñavalkya Smṛti and echoed in the Purāṇas: **offer each graha flowers of its own colour**. Every flower list you meet in a temple, a pañcāṅga or a WhatsApp forward is an application of that rule — and the most beautiful clues to it are woven into a hymn most of us chant without noticing: Vyāsa's *Navagraha Stotra*.

I began this as a simple question. Why does one priest offer red lotus to Sūrya and another offer arka (white crown flower)? Why is Śani given blue water-lily in one temple and aparājitā in another? The answer, once traced to the texts, turns out to be more elegant than any single list.

This article follows the trail in three steps: the dharmaśāstra rule, the botanical code inside the stotra, and then each graha in turn — with its flower, its śloka, and the story behind it. Throughout, I mark clearly what is **classical text**, what is **later commentary or temple custom**, and what is **interpretation**.

## The master key: Yājñavalkya's rule of colour

The oldest systematic instruction on how to worship the nine grahas sits in the **Yājñavalkya Smṛti, Ācārādhyāya, Grahaśānti-prakaraṇa (1.295–308)**. It does not name nine flowers. It does something more powerful: it gives the *rule* that generates them.

First, it fixes the nine and their material bodies:

> सूर्यः सोमो महीपुत्रः सोमपुत्रो बृहस्पतिः । शुक्रः शनैश्चरो राहुः केतुश्चेति ग्रहाः स्मृताः ॥ *(Yāj. 1.296)*
>
> *sūryaḥ somo mahīputraḥ somaputro bṛhaspatiḥ / śukraḥ śanaiścaro rāhuḥ ketuś ceti grahāḥ smṛtāḥ*

> ताम्रकात् स्फटिकाद् रक्तचन्दनात् स्वर्णकादुभौ । राजतादयसः सीसात् कांस्यात् कार्या ग्रहाः क्रमात् ॥ *(Yāj. 1.297)*
>
> *tāmrakāt sphaṭikād raktacandanāt svarṇakād ubhau / rājatād ayasaḥ sīsāt kāṃsyāt kāryā grahāḥ kramāt*

The images are to be made, in order, of copper (Sūrya), crystal (Candra), red sandalwood (Maṅgala), gold for both Budha and Guru, silver (Śukra), iron (Śani), lead (Rāhu) and bronze (Ketu). Notice: each material already *looks like* its graha.

Then comes the line that governs every flower list ever written:

> स्ववर्णैर्वा पटे लेख्या गन्धैर्मण्डलकेषु वा । **यथावर्णं प्रदेयानि वासांसि कुसुमानि च** ॥ *(Yāj. 1.298)*
>
> *svavarṇair vā paṭe lekhyā gandhair maṇḍalakeṣu vā / yathāvarṇaṃ pradeyāni vāsāṃsi kusumāni ca*

*"Or they may be painted on cloth in their own colours, or drawn in maṇḍalas with fragrant pastes. Garments and flowers are to be offered according to each one's colour."*

**Yathāvarṇam kusumāni** — flowers by colour. That is the whole secret. The text deliberately leaves the species open, because the right flower is whatever blooms in your region in the graha's hue. This is why a Kashmiri, a Bengali and a Tamil priest can all be "correct" with different flowers.

The same passage gives the nine sacred woods (samidh) by name, and these *are* fixed:

> अर्कः पलाशः खदिर अपामार्गोऽथ पिप्पलः । उदुम्बरः शमी दूर्वा कुशाश्च समिधः क्रमात् ॥ *(Yāj. 1.302)*
>
> *arkaḥ palāśaḥ khadira apāmārgo 'tha pippalaḥ / udumbaraḥ śamī dūrvā kuśāś ca samidhaḥ kramāt*

Arka for Sūrya, palāśa for Candra, khadira for Maṅgala, apāmārga for Budha, pippala for Guru, udumbara for Śukra, śamī for Śani, dūrvā for Rāhu, kuśa for Ketu. Several of these plants also flower — and as we will see, two of them (arka and palāśa) quietly became the most "classical" graha flowers of all.

Finally, the rationale for the whole rite, in two verses every jyotiṣī should know by heart:

> यश्च यस्य यदा दुःस्थः स तं यत्नेन पूजयेत् ॥ *(Yāj. 1.307a)*
>
> *yaś ca yasya yadā duḥsthaḥ sa taṃ yatnena pūjayet*

*"Whichever graha is ill-placed for a person at a given time, him he should worship with special effort."*

> ग्रहाधीना नरेन्द्राणामुच्छ्रायाः पतनानि च । भावाभावौ च जगतस्तस्मात् पूज्यतमा ग्रहाः ॥ *(Yāj. 1.308)*
>
> *grahādhīnā narendrāṇām ucchrāyāḥ patanāni ca / bhāvābhāvau ca jagatas tasmāt pūjyatamā grahāḥ*

*"The rise and fall of kings, the being and non-being of the world, depend on the grahas — therefore the grahas are most worthy of worship."*

So the dharmaśāstra position is clear: the flower is not a magic key in itself. It is an act of **sādṛśya** — offering the graha its own likeness, so that the offering resonates with the deity's nature.

## The hidden botany in the Navagraha Stotra and Parāśara

If Yājñavalkya gives the rule, where are the colours to apply it? Two sources answer — one a jyotiṣa śāstra, the other a hymn.

**Parāśara's colour code.** The *Bṛhat Parāśara Horā Śāstra*, chapter 3, describes the grahas' bodies:

> रक्तश्यामो दिवाधीशो गौरगात्रो निशाकरः । नात्युच्चाङ्गः कुजो रक्तो दूर्वाश्यामो बुधस्तथा ॥ *(BPHS 3.16)*
>
> *raktaśyāmo divādhīśo gauragātro niśākaraḥ / nātyuccāṅgaḥ kujo rakto dūrvāśyāmo budhas tathā*

> गौरगात्रो गुरुर्ज्ञेयः शुक्रः श्यावस्तथैव च । कृष्णदेहो रवेः पुत्रो ज्ञायते द्विजसत्तम ॥ *(BPHS 3.17)*
>
> *gauragātro gurur jñeyaḥ śukraḥ śyāvas tathaiva ca / kṛṣṇadeho raveḥ putro jñāyate dvijasattama*

Sun: red-dark. Moon: fair. Mars: red. Mercury: dark-green like **dūrvā grass**. Jupiter: fair-golden. Venus: dusky. Saturn: black. And of the nodes:

> धूम्राकारो नीलतनुः … स्वर्भानुस्तत्समः शिखी ॥ *(BPHS 3.30)*
>
> *dhūmrākāro nīlatanuḥ … svarbhānus tatsamaḥ śikhī*

Rāhu is smoke-coloured and blue-bodied; Ketu is like him.

Two further lines in the same chapter are, in my view, the most overlooked flower references in all of jyotiṣa:

> सर्वात्मा च दिवानाथो मनः **कुमुदबान्धवः** ॥ *(BPHS 3.12a)*
>
> *sarvātmā ca divānātho manaḥ kumudabāndhavaḥ*

The Moon is the mind — and is named **kumuda-bāndhava, "kinsman of the night water-lily"**. The kumuda opens only under moonlight. Parāśara is not just naming Candra; he is telling you his flower.

> **पुष्पवृक्षं भृगोः पुत्रे** … ॥ *(BPHS 3.40a)*
>
> *puṣpavṛkṣaṃ bhṛgoḥ putre …*

Among the grahas as lords of plant life, **flowering plants (puṣpa-vṛkṣa) belong to Śukra**. Venus is the kāraka of flowers themselves. Every flower offering, to any graha, passes through Śukra's domain — which is why a weak Śukra is traditionally strengthened with fragrant white blossoms and why flower-trade and perfumery are read from Venus.

**The Navagraha Stotra's flower similes.** The nine-verse hymn attributed to Vyāsa is chanted daily across India. Read it as a botanist and something remarkable appears: **four of its nine dhyāna lines describe the graha by comparing him to a flower or plant.**

> जपाकुसुमसङ्काशं काश्यपेयं महाद्युतिम् । तमोऽरिं सर्वपापघ्नं प्रणतोऽस्मि दिवाकरम् ॥
>
> *japākusumasaṅkāśaṃ kāśyapeyaṃ mahādyutim / tamo'riṃ sarvapāpaghnaṃ praṇato'smi divākaram*

Sūrya "resembles the **japā flower**" — the red hibiscus.

> प्रियङ्गुकलिकाश्यामं रूपेणाप्रतिमं बुधम् । सौम्यं सौम्यगुणोपेतं तं बुधं प्रणमाम्यहम् ॥
>
> *priyaṅgukalikāśyāmaṃ rūpeṇāpratimaṃ budham / saumyaṃ saumyaguṇopetaṃ taṃ budhaṃ praṇamāmy aham*

Budha is "dark-green like the **bud of the priyaṅgu**" — the same dusky green Parāśara calls dūrvā-śyāma.

> हिमकुन्दमृणालाभं दैत्यानां परमं गुरुम् । सर्वशास्त्रप्रवक्तारं भार्गवं प्रणमाम्यहम् ॥
>
> *himakundamṛṇālābhaṃ daityānāṃ paramaṃ gurum / sarvaśāstrapravaktāraṃ bhārgavaṃ praṇamāmy aham*

Śukra "shines like snow, the **kunda jasmine** and the **lotus stalk (mṛṇāla)**."

> पलाशपुष्पसङ्काशं तारकाग्रहमस्तकम् । रौद्रं रौद्रात्मकं घोरं तं केतुं प्रणमाम्यहम् ॥
>
> *palāśapuṣpasaṅkāśaṃ tārakāgrahamastakam / raudraṃ raudrātmakaṃ ghoraṃ taṃ ketuṃ praṇamāmy aham*

Ketu "resembles the **palāśa flower**" — the flame-of-the-forest.

The other five verses use non-floral images that still encode colour: Candra like curd, conch and frost; Maṅgala with the brilliance of lightning; Guru like gold (kāñcana-sannibham); Śani like blue-black collyrium (nīlāñjana-samābhāsam); and Rāhu as the half-bodied eclipser of Sun and Moon.

Put the stotra beside Yājñavalkya and the method is complete. The smṛti says *offer by colour*; the stotra and Parāśara say *here is each colour, often already in the shape of a flower*.

## The nine grahas and their flowers

For each graha I give the textual basis first, then the flowers used in practice, then the story or reasoning that binds them. Where a flower comes from regional temple custom rather than a Sanskrit text, I say so.

### 1. Sūrya — japā (red hibiscus), red lotus, arka

**Textual basis.** The Navagraha Stotra's first line: *japākusumasaṅkāśam*, "like the japā flower". BPHS 3.16 gives his colour as rakta-śyāma, red-dark. Yājñavalkya 1.302 makes **arka** (Calotropis, crown flower) his sacred wood.

**Flowers in practice.** Red hibiscus and red lotus everywhere; arka flowers and leaves, especially on Ratha Saptamī. Sūrya's images traditionally hold two full-blown red lotuses.

**The story.** The lotus opens at sunrise and closes at sunset — it is the one flower that literally obeys the Sun, which is why Sūrya is *padma-bandhu*, friend of the lotus. Arka carries his very name: *arka* is itself a Sanskrit word for the Sun. In Tamil temple lore, the Suryanar Koil near Kumbakonam stands where the grahas, cursed by Brahmā for overstepping their authority, were sent down to **Vellerukku-vanam, the forest of white arka**, and worshipped Śiva there to be released. The Sun's own plant became the grahas' place of penance.

### 2. Candra — kumuda (night water-lily), white lotus, white flowers

**Textual basis.** BPHS 3.12 calls him *kumuda-bāndhava*, kinsman of the night-lily. The stotra describes him as *dadhi-śaṅkha-tuṣārābham*, white as curd, conch and frost. His samidh is **palāśa** (Yāj. 1.302).

**Flowers in practice.** White water-lily, white lotus, white oleander (Tamil *arali*), jasmine and any white bloom on Mondays.

**The story.** The kumuda is the mirror of the lotus: it sleeps by day and opens under the Moon. Sanskrit poets call the Moon *kumuda-pati*, "lord of the night-lilies". Where the Sun's flower answers the light of the soul (ātmā), the Moon's flower answers the cool reflected light of the mind (manas) — exactly the pairing BPHS 3.12 makes in a single line.

### 3. Maṅgala — red flowers: red oleander, red champak, red lotus

**Textual basis.** BPHS 3.16: *kujo raktaḥ*, Mars is red. The stotra: born of the Earth's womb, with the brilliance of lightning. Yājñavalkya assigns his image to **red sandalwood** and his samidh to **khadira**. Classical lexica list *raktasāra*, "red-cored", as a synonym of khadira.

**Flowers in practice.** Red karavīra (oleander), red hibiscus, red lotus; in Tamil Nadu, *shenbagam* (champak) is widely offered to Aṅgāraka.

**The story.** Everything about Maṅgala is blood and fire — *aṅgāraka*, the glowing coal. His wood has a red heart, his image is cut from red sandal, and his flowers are red. Note the logic: Maṅgala's ritual set is a single colour carried through every material, the clearest example of Yājñavalkya's *yathāvarṇam* rule.

### 4. Budha — priyaṅgu, dūrvā-green and leafy offerings

**Textual basis.** The stotra: *priyaṅgu-kalikā-śyāmam*, dark-green like the priyaṅgu bud. BPHS 3.16: *dūrvā-śyāmaḥ*, dark like dūrvā grass. His samidh is **apāmārga** (Yāj. 1.302).

**Flowers in practice.** This is where lists disagree most. Green leaves (tulasī, dūrvā), white flowers, and in some North Indian practice yellow flowers. The *Matsya Purāṇa*'s graha-śānti chapter visualises Budha and Guru together as yellow, which explains the yellow variant.

**The story.** Budha is the son of Candra and Tārā, the prince among grahas — young, quick, verdant. Green is the colour of growth, of things not yet ripened. His "flower" is fittingly a *bud* (kalikā) rather than an open bloom: the planet of youth gets the unopened flower. That single word in the stotra is, I think, the most elegant piece of graha symbolism in the hymn.

### 5. Guru — yellow flowers: champak, yellow chrysanthemum, jasmine

**Textual basis.** The stotra: *kāñcana-sannibham*, like gold. BPHS 3.43 assigns him the **pītāmbara**, yellow garment. His samidh is **pippala**, the aśvattha (Yāj. 1.302); his image is of gold (Yāj. 1.297).

**Flowers in practice.** Yellow champaka, yellow chrysanthemum (*sevanti*), marigold; in Tamil temples *mullai* (jasmine) for Guru, and yellow garlands on Thursdays.

**The story.** Guru is the teacher of the devas; the aśvattha is the tree of the eternal Veda (the Gītā's upside-down aśvattha whose leaves are the hymns). His flowers carry the colour of gold and of ripened grain — knowledge that has matured into fruit, in contrast to Budha's bud.

### 6. Śukra — kunda jasmine, white lotus, fragrant white flowers

**Textual basis.** The stotra: *hima-kunda-mṛṇālābham*, like snow, kunda jasmine and lotus stalk. BPHS 3.40: **flowering plants belong to Śukra**. His image is silver (Yāj. 1.297), his samidh **udumbara** (Yāj. 1.302).

**Flowers in practice.** Kunda and mallikā jasmine, white lotus, white rose, any strongly fragrant white flower on Fridays.

**The story.** Śukra is the guru of the asuras and the master of *mṛta-sañjīvanī*, the knowledge that revives the dead. He is also beauty, desire and refinement. If every graha has a flower, Śukra *owns* flowering itself. The poet's choice of kunda — a jasmine that blooms in the cold season, white as snow — joins Venus's purity of colour to its love of fragrance.

### 7. Śani — blue lotus, aparājitā, dark-blue flowers, śamī

**Textual basis.** The stotra: *nīlāñjana-samābhāsam*, like blue-black collyrium. BPHS 3.17: *kṛṣṇa-dehaḥ*, dark-bodied. His image is iron, his samidh **śamī** (Yāj. 1.297, 1.302).

**Flowers in practice.** Blue water-lily (*nīlotpala*; Tamil *karunkuvalai*), blue aparājitā (butterfly pea), dark-blue or violet flowers; śamī leaves and flowers on Saturdays.

**The story.** No Sanskrit text gives Śani a named flower — and that silence is itself telling. Śani is the slow, ascetic, austere graha; BPHS 3.40 even calls him lord of the *nīrasa*, the sapless and dry. Yet tradition chose the **aparājitā**, "the unconquered", a vine that keeps flowering through poor soil and neglect. For the planet of endurance, that is a precise choice. Śamī, his wood, is the tree of victory worshipped on Vijayadaśamī.

### 8. Rāhu — dark and smoky flowers, dūrvā

**Textual basis.** BPHS 3.30: *dhūmrākāro nīlatanuḥ*, smoke-coloured and blue-bodied. His image is lead (Yāj. 1.297); his samidh is **dūrvā** grass (Yāj. 1.302). The stotra gives no colour simile, only *ardhakāyam* — half-bodied.

**Flowers in practice.** Dark-blue or smoky-hued flowers; in South Indian custom, *mandārai* (Bauhinia) is often given; dūrvā grass is offered in place of a flower.

**The story.** Svarbhānu drank the amṛta disguised among the devas, and Viṣṇu's discus severed him into head (Rāhu) and body (Ketu). A graha with no body, who eclipses the two lights, is given **dūrvā** — a grass that is cut endlessly yet always returns from its root. The fitness is uncanny: Rāhu too was cut, and yet he endures.

### 9. Ketu — palāśa (flame-of-the-forest), red water-lily, multi-coloured flowers

**Textual basis.** The stotra: *palāśa-puṣpa-saṅkāśam*, like the palāśa flower. Classical lexica list *raktapuṣpaka*, "red-flowered", as a synonym of palāśa. His image is bronze, his samidh **kuśa** (Yāj. 1.297, 1.302).

**Flowers in practice.** Palāśa flowers where available; red *alli* (red water-lily) in Tamil temples; flowers of mixed colours, matching Ketu's *citra* (variegated) nature.

**The story.** The palāśa blooms on bare branches before its leaves return — a tree on fire with no foliage. For Ketu, the body without a head, the graha of detachment and mokṣa, that image is exact: blazing flower, no worldly covering. Kuśa, his samidh, is the purifying grass of every Vedic rite — the material of renunciation.

One quiet pattern emerges. **Candra's wood (palāśa) is Ketu's flower.** The palāśa is the Vedic *brahma-vṛkṣa*, the tree of the sacrifice. I leave the reader to sit with why the mind's wood should be the flower of liberation.

## What is classical, and what is not

A research note owes its readers a clean line between text and custom. Here is mine.

**Classical and verifiable:**

- The rule of offering flowers and cloth by each graha's colour — Yājñavalkya Smṛti 1.298.
- The nine images' materials, nine samidhs, nine naivedyas and nine dakṣiṇās — Yājñavalkya Smṛti 1.297–306.
- Graha colours — BPHS 3.16–17 and 3.30; similar lists in the Purāṇas.
- Candra as *kumuda-bāndhava* and Śukra as lord of flowering plants — BPHS 3.12, 3.40.
- The flower similes for Sūrya (japā), Budha (priyaṅgu bud), Śukra (kunda, mṛṇāla) and Ketu (palāśa) — Navagraha Stotra attributed to Vyāsa.

**Later or regional practice:**

- Fixed one-flower-per-graha lists (e.g. *shenbagam* for Mars, *mullai* for Guru, *karunkuvalai* for Śani, *mandārai* for Rāhu, red *alli* for Ketu). These reflect Āgamic temple practice, especially in Tamil Nadu's Navagraha circuit, and vary between temples.
- Assigning blue flowers to Śani and Rāhu. This applies the colour rule faithfully, but no Sanskrit text I have found names a specific flower for either.

**Where sources disagree:**

- Budha's colour is green (BPHS, stotra) in jyotiṣa, but yellow in the Matsya Purāṇa's graha-śānti. Both are defensible; follow your sampradāya.
- Śukra is *śyāva* (dusky) in BPHS but white in the stotra and in almost all worship. Ritual practice follows the stotra.

**On "secret texts".** I went looking for a hidden manuscript with the true navagraha flower list. There isn't one — and anyone selling you one should be asked for the verse number. The real secret is better: a two-word rule (*yathāvarṇaṃ kusumāni*) that lets a devotee anywhere in the world choose the right flower from their own garden. That is not a lost teaching. It is a teaching most people walk past.

## Closing: the flower as likeness

The ṛṣis did not hand us a shopping list. They handed us a principle of resonance — offer the graha his own likeness, in metal, in cloth, in wood and in bloom — and then hid the colour key in a hymn we chant every morning. Sūrya's hibiscus, Budha's unopened bud, Śukra's snow-white jasmine and Ketu's leafless flame-tree are not arbitrary. Each is a portrait.

Yājñavalkya's closing promise for those who keep this hospitality of the grahas is worth ending on:

> ग्रहाणामिदमातिथ्यं कुर्यात् संवत्सरादपि । आरोग्यबलसंपन्नो जीवेत् स शरदः शतम् ॥ *(Yāj. 1.308A)*
>
> *grahāṇām idam ātithyaṃ kuryāt saṃvatsarād api / ārogyabalasaṃpanno jīvet sa śaradaḥ śatam*

*"Let one offer this hospitality to the grahas even once a year; endowed with health and strength, he lives a hundred autumns."*
