*Sequel to [Part A: The Flowers of the Nine Grahas](/posts/navagraha-flowers-part-a/).*

Part A found that the grahas receive flowers by one rule: **offer each its own colour**. The devatās work differently. Their flowers are fixed by **story** — a boon, a curse, a birth, a battle. Gaṇeśa gets dūrvā because of a fire he swallowed; Śiva refuses ketakī because it lied; Viṣṇu wears tulasī because of a woman's devotion and his own deceit. To understand the flower is to remember the myth.

## Begin with a surprise: the Vedic gods received almost no flowers

Read the Ṛgveda looking for flower offerings and you will come back nearly empty-handed. Indra, Agni, Varuṇa and Soma were fed through the fire — ghee, grain, soma juice, cakes. Flowers as a primary offering belong to **pūjā**, the later worship of an image, codified in the Gṛhyasūtras, Āgamas and Purāṇas. That is a historical fact worth stating plainly before quoting a single verse.

And yet, the flower is inside the Veda — as a teaching. The **Mantrapuṣpam**, chanted at the close of almost every pūjā in South India, comes from the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka (1.22) of the Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda:

> यो॑ऽपां पुष्पं॒ वेद॑ । पुष्प॑वान् प्र॒जावा॑न् पशु॒मान् भ॑वति । च॒न्द्रमा॒ वा अ॒पां पुष्प॑म् ॥
>
> *yo 'pāṃ puṣpaṃ veda / puṣpavān prajāvān paśumān bhavati / candramā vā apāṃ puṣpam*

*"Whoever knows the flower of the waters becomes rich in flowers, in offspring, in cattle. The Moon, truly, is the flower of the waters."*

The Veda's "flower" is the Moon blooming on the cosmic ocean — the visible essence of an invisible source. The hymn goes on to name fire, wind, sun, stars, clouds and the year as each other's resting-places (*āyatana*). This is why priests offer a physical flower while chanting it: the petal stands for the essence that rises from the depths.

Hold on to that idea — **the flower is the essence made visible**. Every story that follows is a variation of it.

## The four great prohibitions

Before any "what to offer", every pūjā tradition teaches "what never to offer". Four rules are known to almost every household priest:

| Never offer | To | Why (the story) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tulasī | Gaṇeśa | Tulasī's curse and Gaṇeśa's counter-curse (below) |
| Ketakī (screw-pine) | Śiva | Ketakī bore false witness for Brahmā (Śiva Purāṇa) |
| Akṣata (unbroken rice) | Viṣṇu | Customary; Vaiṣṇava pūjā uses tulasī in its place |
| Dūrvā grass | Devī | Customary; dūrvā is Gaṇeśa's own |

One honest note. These four are usually quoted as a single Sanskrit verse, but I could not trace that verse to a named text. The Gaṇeśa–tulasī and Śiva–ketakī rules rest on Purāṇic stories; the other two are living ritual custom. That makes them no less binding in practice — it only means the reader should not be told they come from the Veda.

Even these rules bend. In the 21-leaf Ekaviṃśati-patra pūjā on Gaṇeśa Caturthī, many traditions include one tulasī leaf, once a year. And the Śiva Purāṇa itself softens the ketakī curse, as we will see.

## Gaṇapati — dūrvā, śamī, mandāra and red flowers

**The textual core.** The *Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa* gives both his colour and his offering in one meditation verse:

> रक्तं लम्बोदरं शूर्पकर्णकं रक्तवाससम् । रक्तगन्धानुलिप्ताङ्गं **रक्तपुष्पैः सुपूजितम्** ॥
>
> *raktaṃ lambodaraṃ śūrpakarṇakaṃ raktavāsasam / raktagandhānuliptāṅgaṃ raktapuṣpaiḥ supūjitam*

*"Red, pot-bellied, with winnowing-fan ears, clad in red, his limbs anointed with red paste, well-worshipped with red flowers."*

Then, in its phala-śruti, the same Upaniṣad names his signature offering:

> **यो दूर्वाङ्कुरैर्यजति स वैश्रवणोपमो भवति** ॥
>
> *yo dūrvāṅkurair yajati sa vaiśravaṇopamo bhavati*

*"Whoever worships with dūrvā sprouts becomes like Vaiśravaṇa (Kubera, lord of wealth)."*

So the two pillars of Gaṇeśa's flower worship — **red flowers** and **dūrvā** — are not folk custom. They are in a text recited at the start of nearly every Vedic-style pūjā.

**Why dūrvā: the story of Analāsura.** In the Gaṇeśa traditions, a fire-demon, Anala, was scorching the three worlds. Gaṇeśa swallowed him whole. The fire raged in his belly and nothing cooled it — not the Moon placed on his head (hence *bhālacandra*), not Viṣṇu's lotus, not Śiva's serpent. Then the ṛṣis placed twenty-one blades of dūrvā on his head, and the burning ceased. Gaṇeśa declared that whoever offers him dūrvā pleases him more than any gold. This is why dūrvā is offered in bunches of 21 (or in pairs, *dūrvā-yugma*), with the tips pointing toward him.

The symbolism runs deep. Dūrvā is the grass that cannot be destroyed — mown, grazed and trampled, it returns from the root. The Remover of Obstacles is given the plant that is itself unstoppable.

**Why śamī and mandāra.** A Gaṇeśa Purāṇa tradition tells of Śamikā, daughter of sage Aurva, and her husband Mandāra, who mocked the elephant-faced sage Bhuśuṇḍi and were cursed to become trees. Gaṇeśa, moved by their devotion, chose to dwell in both. Hence the **śamī** leaf and **mandāra** (white Erythrina / or Calotropis in some regions) flower are dear to him, and a Gaṇeśa image carved from mandāra root is especially prized.

**Why not tulasī.** In the Purāṇic story (told in the Brahmavaivarta tradition), the young Tulasī saw Gaṇeśa in meditation on the banks of the Gaṅgā and proposed marriage. Gaṇeśa, a brahmacārī, refused. Stung, she cursed him to marry; he cursed her to become a plant. When she repented, he softened it: she would be dearest to Viṣṇu and the giver of mokṣa — but never part of his own worship.

**The 21 leaves (Ekaviṃśati-patra).** On Gaṇeśa Caturthī, he is worshipped with 21 kinds of leaves. Lists differ by region, but commonly include śamī, bilva, dūrvā, dhattūra, badarī (jujube), apāmārga, mango, karavīra, arka, aśvattha, arjuna, devadāru and pomegranate. Ayurvedic writers note that most are medicinal plants of the monsoon season, when the festival falls — a pūjā that doubles as a herbarium.

**In practice:** red hibiscus (japā), red lotus, dūrvā in 21s, śamī, mandāra, arka garlands; modaka on the side.

## Śiva — bilva, dhattūra, arka, white flowers, and the banished ketakī

**Bilva: the leaf above all flowers.** For Śiva the supreme offering is not a flower at all but the three-fold leaf of the bilva (Aegle marmelos). The Śiva Purāṇa (Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā) devotes a whole māhātmya to it, and the popular *Bilvāṣṭakam* condenses its theology into one line:

> त्रिदलं त्रिगुणाकारं त्रिनेत्रं च त्रियायुधम् । त्रिजन्मपापसंहारं एकबिल्वं शिवार्पणम् ॥
>
> *tridalaṃ triguṇākāraṃ trinetraṃ ca triyāyudham / trijanmapāpasaṃhāraṃ ekabilvaṃ śivārpaṇam*

*"Three leaflets, shaped as the three guṇas, the three eyes, the three-pronged weapon — one bilva offered to Śiva destroys the sins of three births."*

The leaf is a portrait of Śiva himself: three eyes, trident, master of the three guṇas. Remember Part A's principle of *likeness* — here it returns, not in colour but in **form**.

**The hunter's night.** The Śiva Purāṇa tells of a hunter who climbed a bilva tree on a moonless night to watch for prey, and, keeping himself awake, plucked and dropped leaves. Unknown to him, they fell on a liṅga below, along with drops of his water. It was Śivarātri. That unintended pūjā earned him liberation. The moral the Purāṇa draws: bilva on Śivarātri works even without knowledge — how much more with it.

**Dhattūra and arka: the poison-drinker's flowers.** Śiva takes what others reject — the white trumpet of dhattūra, the milky crown-flower of arka, the poisonous karavīra (oleander). The mythic logic is the *samudra-manthana*: when the hālāhala poison rose from the churned ocean, Śiva drank it and held it in his throat, becoming Nīlakaṇṭha. The god who swallowed the world's poison is offered the world's poisonous flowers. No other deity accepts these.

**White and the colour of camphor.** Śiva's dhyāna is *karpūra-gauram*, white as camphor. Hence white flowers — jasmine, white arka, white lotus, kunda — dominate Śaiva worship, with **kumuda** for the Moon on his crest.

**The banished ketakī.** In the Śiva Purāṇa, Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā chapter 8, Brahmā and Viṣṇu quarrel over who is greater. A column of fire appears with no end. Viṣṇu, as a boar, digs down and honestly admits failure. Brahmā, as a swan, flies up, meets a ketakī flower falling from above, and persuades it to testify that he reached the top. Śiva emerges from the column and rebukes the liar. To the ketakī he says it is not fit to be worn by him, for he is a speaker of truth. When the ketakī pleads, he relents only partly: it may adorn the canopy over him, but not his body.

The lesson is precise: **beauty and fragrance cannot redeem a false witness**. The most fragrant flower of the Indian monsoon is shut out from the god of truth.

**The thousand lotuses.** In another Śiva Purāṇa episode, Viṣṇu vows to worship Śiva with a thousand lotuses daily. Śiva, testing him, hides one. Finding the count short, Viṣṇu — the lotus-eyed (*kamalākṣa*) — plucks out his own eye to complete the offering. Śiva, overwhelmed, gives him the Sudarśana cakra. Here the flower and the devotee's own body become interchangeable.

**In practice:** bilva above all; dhattūra, arka, white karavīra, white lotus, jasmine, kumuda; never ketakī (except as canopy, and on Śivarātri in some traditions).

## Viṣṇu and his avatāras — tulasī, the lotus, and the eight flowers of the heart

**The lotus is his body.** Viṣṇu does not merely receive lotuses; his names are made of them. The Viṣṇu Sahasranāma calls him *padmanābha* (lotus-navelled), *puṣkarākṣa* (lotus-eyed) and *padmī padmanibhekṣaṇaḥ* (holder of the lotus, with lotus-like eyes). From his navel rises the lotus on which Brahmā is born; in his hand he holds the padma beside conch, discus and mace. To offer Viṣṇu a lotus is to return a part of himself.

**Tulasī: the leaf he cannot do without.** Every Vaiṣṇava pūjā is incomplete without tulasī. The *Tulasī Stotra* puts it bluntly:

> तुलस्याः पल्लवं विष्णोः शिरस्यारोपितं कलौ । आरोपयति सर्वाणि श्रेयांसि वरमस्तके ॥
>
> *tulasyāḥ pallavaṃ viṣṇoḥ śirasy āropitaṃ kalau / āropayati sarvāṇi śreyāṃsi varamastake*

*"In the Kali age, a tulasī sprig placed on Viṣṇu's head places every blessing on the head of the one who offers it."*

The Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa explains her name in Viṣṇu's own words — she is *Tulasī* because she has no *tulā*, no equal, in all the worlds.

**The story behind the leaf.** Two Purāṇic versions exist. In the Śaiva-Padma telling, **Vṛndā**, the faithful wife of the asura Jalandhara, made her husband invincible through her chastity. To end Jalandhara, Viṣṇu took his form and deceived her. When she discovered it, she cursed Viṣṇu to become a stone — the **śālagrāma** — and gave up her body; from her ashes grew the tulasī. In the Devī Bhāgavata version (Skandha 9), she is Tulasī, wife of Śaṅkhacūḍa, and Lakṣmī herself in partial form; the curse and counter-curse make her both the Gaṇḍakī river and the tulasī plant, and Viṣṇu the śālagrāma stone found in that river.

Either way the logic is the same: **the plant is a woman whose devotion out-ranked the god's own trickery**. Viṣṇu accepts her as his eternal ornament — and every year on Prabodhinī Ekādaśī the Tulasī-vivāha re-marries them.

**The eight flowers of the heart.** Vaiṣṇava teachers cite a verse, quoted by Vātsya Varadācārya in his *Prapanna-pārijāta* and often attributed to the Purāṇas, that names the flowers Viṣṇu truly wants:

> अहिंसा प्रथमं पुष्पं पुष्पमिन्द्रियनिग्रहः । सर्वभूतदया पुष्पं क्षमा पुष्पं विशेषतः । ज्ञानं पुष्पं तपः पुष्पं ध्यानं पुष्पं तथैव च । सत्यमष्टविधं पुष्पं विष्णोः प्रीतिकरं भवेत् ॥
>
> *ahiṃsā prathamaṃ puṣpaṃ puṣpam indriyanigrahaḥ / sarvabhūtadayā puṣpaṃ kṣamā puṣpaṃ viśeṣataḥ / jñānaṃ puṣpaṃ tapaḥ puṣpaṃ dhyānaṃ puṣpaṃ tathaiva ca / satyam aṣṭavidhaṃ puṣpaṃ viṣṇoḥ prītikaraṃ bhavet*

*"Non-violence is the first flower; sense-restraint a flower; compassion to all beings a flower; forbearance above all a flower; knowledge, austerity and meditation are flowers; and truth — these eight flowers delight Viṣṇu."*

This is the Mantrapuṣpam's teaching turned inward: the real flower is the essence, and the essence of a devotee is character.

**The avatāras.** Each form inherits Viṣṇu's tulasī and lotus, and adds its own:

- **Kṛṣṇa** — the *vanamālā* (forest garland) and *vaijayantī* garland of the Bhāgavata; the **kadamba** tree of Vṛndāvana, from which he leapt into the Kāliya pool; and the **pārijāta** he carried off from Indra's garden (see below).
- **Rāma** — tulasī and lotus; in Rāmāyaṇa poetry he is *rājīva-locana*, lotus-eyed.
- **Narasiṃha** — red flowers in many temples, for the ugra form; tulasī remains essential.
- **Veṅkaṭeśvara** — Tirumala's daily *pūlaṅgi* garlands of tulasī, jasmine and lotus.

**In practice:** tulasī above all; lotus, jasmine, champaka, pārijāta, kadamba for Kṛṣṇa; akṣata is customarily avoided in Vaiṣṇava pūjā.

## Devī in all her forms — lotus, hibiscus, and the flowers in her hair

The Goddess is one and many. Her flowers follow her forms: **white for Sarasvatī, pink-red lotus for Lakṣmī, blood-red for Durgā and Kālī, the full spring garden for Lalitā**. One rule unites them — dūrvā, Gaṇeśa's grass, is customarily kept off her altar.

### Lakṣmī — the lotus, and a secret about bilva

The *Śrī Sūkta* (a khila hymn appended to the Ṛgveda) is almost entirely made of lotuses:

> पद्मानने पद्मविपद्मपत्रे पद्मप्रिये पद्मदलायताक्षि । विश्वप्रिये विष्णुमनोऽनुकूले त्वत्पादपद्मं मयि सन्निधत्स्व ॥
>
> *padmānane padmavipadmapatre padmapriye padmadalāyatākṣi / viśvapriye viṣṇumano'nukūle tvatpādapadmaṃ mayi sannidhatsva*

*"Lotus-faced, dwelling among lotuses, lover of lotuses, with eyes long as lotus petals, beloved of the world, pleasing to Viṣṇu's mind — place your lotus feet within me."*

She is *padmālayā*, *padmahastā*, *padmamālinī*. The lotus rises clean out of mud — wealth that is not tainted by its source.

Now the hidden detail. The same Śrī Sūkta says:

> आदित्यवर्णे तपसोऽधिजातो वनस्पतिस्तव वृक्षोऽथ बिल्वः ॥
>
> *ādityavarṇe tapaso 'dhijāto vanaspatis tava vṛkṣo 'tha bilvaḥ*

*"O sun-coloured one, from your austerity was born the lord of trees — your tree, the bilva."*

The bilva, Śiva's leaf, is in the Vedic hymn **Lakṣmī's own tree, born of her tapas**. This is why bilva is called *śrīvṛkṣa*, and why the Śaiva and Śākta traditions share it. Few devotees offering bilva to a liṅga know they are offering Lakṣmī's tree.

**In practice:** pink and red lotus, red rose, marigold, champaka; on Fridays and Dīpāvalī, lotus seeds and lotus garlands.

### Sarasvatī — the white lotus

Her best-known dhyāna (from the Sarasvatī Stotra) is a study in white:

> या कुन्देन्दुतुषारहारधवला या शुभ्रवस्त्रावृता । या वीणावरदण्डमण्डितकरा या श्वेतपद्मासना ॥
>
> *yā kundendutuṣārahāradhavalā yā śubhravastrāvṛtā / yā vīṇāvaradaṇḍamaṇḍitakarā yā śvetapadmāsanā*

*"She who is white as kunda jasmine, the moon, frost and a pearl necklace, clad in pure white, her hand adorned with the vīṇā, seated on a white lotus."*

Note the overlap with Part A: Śukra is *hima-kunda-mṛṇālābha* and Sarasvatī is *kundendu-tuṣāra-dhavalā*. The kunda jasmine is the flower of both learning and refinement. **In practice:** white lotus, kunda and mallikā jasmine, white chrysanthemum; on Vasanta Pañcamī, yellow mustard and marigold for spring.

### Durgā — red flowers and the 108 lotuses

Durgā, the warrior, takes red: hibiscus, red lotus, red oleander. The most famous Durgā flower story is Bengali and medieval rather than Sanskrit. In Kṛttivāsa's 15th-century Bengali Rāmāyaṇa, Rāma performs an untimely autumn worship (*akāla-bodhana*) of Durgā before facing Rāvaṇa, vowing 108 blue lotuses. The Goddess hides one. Rāma — called *rājīva-locana*, lotus-eyed — raises an arrow to offer his own eye in its place, and she stops him and grants victory.

Read beside Śiva's thousand-lotus story, the pattern is unmistakable: **the lotus-eyed god offers his eye when the last flower is missing**. Bengal's Durgā Pūjā still offers 108 lotuses at Sandhi Pūjā. In the same festival, the *navapatrikā* — nine plants bound together, including bilva, plantain, turmeric and paddy — is worshipped as the Goddess herself.

### Kālī — japā, the red hibiscus

No flower is more bound to Kālī than **japā**. Red as blood and as the tongue of the Goddess, the hibiscus garland (*jabā-mālā* in Bengali) is the standard offering in every Kālī temple. Bengali devotional poets like Rāmprasād sing of offering the red jabā at her feet. Part A noted Sūrya is *japākusuma-saṅkāśa*; the same flower that pictures the rising Sun pictures the Goddess who devours time.

### Lalitā Tripurasundarī — the whole garden

The *Lalitā Sahasranāma* describes her hair in its 13th name:

> चम्पकाशोकपुन्नागसौगन्धिकलसत्कचा
>
> *campakāśoka-punnāga-saugandhika-lasat-kacā*

*"Her tresses shine with campaka, aśoka, punnāga and saugandhika flowers."*

She also holds a bow of sugarcane and **five flower arrows** (*pañca-tanmātra-sāyakā*) — the arrows of Kāmadeva, which the Sahasranāma reinterprets as the five subtle elements. Her colour is *aruṇa*, the red of dawn. **In practice:** red lotus, red hibiscus, campaka, punnāga, aśoka, saugandhika (red water-lily), kadamba (she dwells in the kadamba forest, *kadamba-vana-vāsinī*).

### Pārvatī and Gaurī

As Śiva's consort she shares his bilva and white flowers; as Gaurī of the Haritālikā and Maṅgaḷā Gaurī vratas, she receives sixteen kinds of leaves and flowers. Kālidāsa's *Kumārasambhava* pictures her approaching Śiva adorned with spring blossoms — aśoka and karṇikāra — the very ornaments Kāmadeva had prepared before Śiva burned him.

## Brahmā — the god born from a flower

Brahmā is the only major deity whose **birth** is a flower. He is *kamalāsana* and *abjaja* — lotus-seated, lotus-born — emerging from the lotus that grows from Viṣṇu's navel at the start of creation. He does not so much receive the lotus as live inside it.

**Puṣkara.** In the Padma Purāṇa's account of creation, Brahmā seeks a place for his sacrifice and lets a lotus fall from his hand; where its petals strike the earth, lakes spring up. The chief of these is Puṣkara in Rajasthan — *puṣkara* itself means "blue lotus" — home to the best-known Brahmā temple in India.

**Why so few temples?** Two flower-linked stories explain it:

1. **The ketakī lie** (Śiva Purāṇa, Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā 8) — Brahmā's false claim, backed by the ketakī, earns Śiva's rebuke that he will not receive worship as other gods do.
2. **Sāvitrī's curse** (Padma Purāṇa tradition) — at the Puṣkara yajña Brahmā's wife Sāvitrī arrived late; another bride, Gāyatrī, was seated in her place, and Sāvitrī cursed him to be worshipped at Puṣkara alone.

The Purāṇas thus tie the creator's near-absence from worship to a flower that lied for him.

**His tree.** Classical lexica name the palāśa *brahma-vṛkṣa*, "Brahmā's tree" — the same flame-of-the-forest that Part A found was Candra's sacred wood and Ketu's flower. The creator, the mind and liberation share one tree.

**In practice:** at Puṣkara, lotuses and white flowers; elsewhere Brahmā is honoured mostly within Viṣṇu or Śiva worship, as the lotus-born seated in Viṣṇu's navel.

## The other devatās

### Skanda / Kārttikeya / Murugan — kadamba and the mountain flowers

In the Tamil Saṅgam poems Murugan is the god of the hills (*kuṟiñci*), and his flowers are the hill flowers: the **kadamba** (he is *Kaṭampaṉ*, "the kadamba-wearer"), the **kāntaḷ** (flame lily, Gloriosa superba) and the **kuṟiñci**, the Strobilanthes that carpets the Western Ghats once in twelve years. As Skanda, the commander of the devas born of fire, he receives red flowers. **In practice:** red flowers, kadamba, kāntaḷ, red oleander; *vel* worship with red.

### Kāmadeva — five flower arrows

The god of love is the only deity whose **weapons are flowers**. A verse widely cited in Sanskrit commentaries lists them:

> अरविन्दमशोकं च चूतं च नवमल्लिका । नीलोत्पलं च पञ्चैते पञ्चबाणस्य सायकाः ॥
>
> *aravindam aśokaṃ ca cūtaṃ ca navamallikā / nīlotpalaṃ ca pañcaite pañcabāṇasya sāyakāḥ*

*"The lotus, aśoka, mango blossom, fresh jasmine and blue water-lily — these five are the arrows of the five-arrowed god."*

These arrows are what Śiva's third eye reduced to ash — and what Lalitā now holds as her own (see above). Kāma is worshipped at Vasanta Pañcamī and Holī with spring flowers, above all **mango blossom**.

### Hanumān — a later, popular deity

Hanumān is not a Vedic god, and no Sanskrit text fixes his flower. Popular practice is consistent, though: red and saffron flowers matching his sindūra, **arka** (ruī) garlands in Maharashtra, and **tulasī** as Rāma's devotee. Mark these as custom.

### Indra — the pārijāta

Indra's flower is the celestial **pārijāta**, one of the treasures from the churning of the ocean, planted in his Nandana garden. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa (Book 5) tells how Kṛṣṇa, at Satyabhāmā's request, carried the tree off from heaven to Dvārakā, defeating Indra in the process. The earthly night-jasmine (*Nyctanthes*), which drops its orange-stemmed flowers at dawn, carries the name. Unlike almost every other flower, fallen pārijāta may be offered — it is said to fall only for worship.

### Kubera — the saugandhika lotus

In the Mahābhārata's Vana Parva, Draupadī asks Bhīma for the fragrant **saugandhika** lotus; he finds it only in the lake of Kubera, lord of wealth, guarded by yakṣas. Recall the Atharvaśīrṣa: dūrvā offered to Gaṇeśa makes one "like Vaiśravaṇa" — like Kubera. Wealth and fragrance share a lake.

### Agni, Varuṇa, Soma — the Vedic gods

Here we return to where we began. These deities are honoured through the fire, not with garlands. Agni's "flowers" are his samidhs — Part A's nine woods; Soma is himself a plant pressed for its juice; Varuṇa, lord of the waters, is the very ocean on which the Mantrapuṣpam's "flower of the waters" floats. To force a flower list onto them would be to invent a tradition. Better to admit what the Veda shows: **for the oldest gods, the offering was the essence itself — ghee, juice, flame — and the flower came later, as its emblem.**

### Sūrya and the grahas

See Part A: japā, red lotus and arka for Sūrya, and colour-matched flowers for each of the nine grahas.

## What is classical, and what is not

**Classical and verifiable:** the Mantrapuṣpam (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 1.22); red flowers and dūrvā for Gaṇeśa (Atharvaśīrṣa); the ketakī curse (Śiva Purāṇa, Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā 8); the lotus epithets of Viṣṇu (Sahasranāma); the lotuses and bilva of the Śrī Sūkta; the flowers in Lalitā's hair (Sahasranāma); Kāma's five arrows; the pārijāta theft (Viṣṇu Purāṇa).

**Purāṇic stories with variant versions:** Gaṇeśa and Analāsura; Tulasī's curse on Gaṇeśa; Vṛndā/Tulasī and the śālagrāma; Śamī and Mandāra; Sāvitrī's curse at Puṣkara.

**Later or regional:** Durgā's 108 lotuses (Bengali, 15th century); japā for Kālī (Bengali Śākta); Hanumān's flowers; the exact 21-leaf list; the akṣata and dūrvā prohibitions.

**On "secret texts" again.** As in Part A, nothing here comes from a hidden manuscript. The deepest finds were in plain sight: that bilva is Lakṣmī's tree in the Śrī Sūkta; that the lotus-eyed god offers his eye in both the Śaiva and Bengali Durgā stories; that the oldest Vedic gods received essences, not flowers. The real secret of pūjā flowers is that **each one is a story compressed into a petal** — and the story is the offering.
