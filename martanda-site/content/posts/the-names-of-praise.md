Every Sanskrit word for a hymn of praise grows from one root, **√stu, "to praise"**. *Stuti* (praise), *stava* (a hymn of praise) and *stotra* are all built on it. What separates the many names — sūkta, stotra, aṣṭakam, kavaca, sahasranāma — is not the act of praising but **where the hymn sits**: in the Vedic sacrifice, in a Purāṇa, or in later devotional poetry, and whether it is named for its number of verses, its purpose or its metre. This note traces each name to the classical text that defines or first uses it, and marks plainly where a name is a later convention.

## One root: √stu

**Pāṇini makes "stotra" a word for an instrument.** The Aṣṭādhyāyī teaches that the suffix *-tra* (ṣṭran) is added to a short list of roots to name the *instrument* of the action:

> दाम्नीशसयुयुजस्तुतुदसिसिचमिहपतदशनहः करणे ॥ *(Aṣṭādhyāyī 3.2.182)*
>
> *dāmnīśasayuyujastutudasisicamihapatadaśanahaḥ karaṇe*

*"[The suffix ṣṭran, from 3.2.181, comes] after dā, nī, śas, yu, yuj, **stu**, tud, si, sic, mih, pat, daś and nah, in the sense of the instrument."*

So **stu + tra = stotra**, "that by which one praises". The same sūtra gives *netra* (the eye, "that by which one leads") from √nī and *śastra* ("that by which one recites") from √śas. A stotra, grammatically, is a tool — the vehicle in which praise travels.

**The Amarakośa treats the words as synonyms.** The standard Sanskrit thesaurus, in its first kāṇḍa (śabdādi-varga), lists them together:

> यशः कीर्तिः समज्ञा च स्तवः स्तोत्रं स्तुतिर्नुतिः ॥ *(Amarakośa, kāṇḍa 1, śabdādi-varga)*
>
> *yaśaḥ kīrtiḥ samajñā ca stavaḥ stotraṃ stutir nutiḥ*

*Stava, stotra, stuti* and *nuti* (from √nu, also "to praise") are given as words of one meaning, beside *yaśas* and *kīrti*, fame. By the time of the lexicon (c. 5th–6th century CE), the distinctions of the older Vedic ritual had worn away and "stotra" simply meant a hymn.

## The Vedic layer: mantra, ṛc, sāman, yajus, sūkta

**Mantra and brāhmaṇa.** The Pūrva Mīmāṃsā Sūtra of Jaimini divides the Veda into two kinds of text. Whatever *calls to mind* what is to be done in the rite is mantra; everything else is brāhmaṇa:

> तच्चोदकेषु मन्त्राख्या ॥ शेषे ब्राह्मणशब्दः ॥ *(Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 2.1.32–33)*
>
> *taccodakeṣu mantrākhyā / śeṣe brāhmaṇaśabdaḥ*

*"The name mantra applies to those [texts] that prompt [the action]; to the rest, the word brāhmaṇa."*

**Ṛc, sāman and yajus.** The very next sūtras give the three classical kinds of mantra, by form:

> तेषामृग्यत्रार्थवशेन पादव्यवस्था ॥ गीतिषु सामाख्या ॥ शेषे यजुःशब्दः ॥ *(Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 2.1.35–37)*
>
> *teṣām ṛg yatrārthavaśena pādavyavasthā / gītiṣu sāmākhyā / śeṣe yajuḥśabdaḥ*

*"Among these, a ṛc is where the division into metrical feet follows the sense; the name sāman is for what is sung; for the rest, the word yajus."*

This is the cleanest classical definition there is:

| Term | Jaimini's test | Where it lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ṛc** | verse in metrical feet (pāda) | Ṛgveda; recited |
| **Sāman** | a ṛc *sung* to a melody | Sāmaveda; chanted |
| **Yajus** | neither verse nor song — ritual prose | Yajurveda; spoken during the action |
| **Nigada** | a loud spoken formula, discussed as a possible fourth kind and folded into yajus (2.1.38–40) | Yajurveda |

The Upaniṣads say the same in their own way. The Chāndogya calls the ṛc the earth and the sāman fire, and explains that the sāman "rests upon" the ṛc — which is why a sāman is sung *on* a ṛc (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 1.6.1). The Bṛhadāraṇyaka puns on the word itself: *sā ca amaś ca*, "she [the ṛc] and he [the melody]" together make sāman (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.3.22).

**Sūkta.** A sūkta is a complete hymn, a set of ṛcs with one seer and usually one deity — the unit in which the Ṛgveda is arranged (1,028 sūktas in ten maṇḍalas). The word is *su + ukta*, "well spoken". It is the proper name for a Vedic hymn: *Puruṣa Sūkta*, *Nāsadīya Sūkta*, *Śrī Sūkta*. Later devotional hymns are not called sūktas.

**What makes a mantra a praise.** Yāska's *Nirukta*, the Vedic science of meaning, opens its book on the deities by defining a mantra's devatā through the act of praise:

> यत्काम ऋषिर्यस्यां देवतायामार्थपत्यमिच्छन् स्तुतिं प्रयुङ्क्ते तद्दैवतः स मन्त्रो भवति ॥ *(Nirukta 7.1)*
>
> *yatkāma ṛṣir yasyāṃ devatāyām ārthapatyam icchan stutiṃ prayuṅkte taddaivataḥ sa mantro bhavati*

*"Whatever deity the seer praises, wishing for some object from that deity, of that deity the mantra is."*

Yāska then sorts the ṛcs into three kinds: those addressed to the deity in the third person (*parokṣakṛta*), those addressed directly as "you" (*pratyakṣakṛta*), and those in which the speaker speaks of himself (*ādhyātmika*). For the Veda, then, **stuti is the defining act**: a hymn belongs to whoever it praises.

## Stotra and śastra: the original meaning

In the soma sacrifice, "stotra" was not a general word for a hymn. It was **a precise ritual unit**, and it always came with a partner.

- A **stotra** is praise **sung** by the udgātṛ priests from the Sāmaveda.
- A **śastra** (from √śaṃs, "to recite, to praise") is praise **recited**, not sung, by the hotṛ and his assistants from the Ṛgveda.

Each stotra is answered by a śastra. In Eggeling's notes to the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (4.3.2.1), the Agniṣṭoma, the model soma rite, has **twelve stotras and twelve śastras**, beginning with the Bahiṣpavamāna-stotra answered by the Ājya-śastra at the morning pressing.

The Mīmāṃsā Sūtra treats the pair as one category and debates its status. It opens with the view that stotra and śastra merely *consecrate* the deity they name, like a yājyā verse:

> स्तुतशस्त्रयोस्तु संस्कारो याज्यावद्देवताभिधानत्वात् ॥ *(Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 2.1.13)*
>
> *stutaśastrayos tu saṃskāro yājyāvad devatābhidhānatvāt*

*"Stuta and śastra are a consecration, like the yājyā, because they name the deity."*

The sūtras that follow (2.1.14–17) argue the opposite, and the tradition reads them as concluding that stotra and śastra are **acts in their own right** (pradhāna-karma), not mere aids. In the oldest technical usage, then, a stotra is something you *do* in the sacrifice.

### The terms around stotra

| Term | Meaning | Classical source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stoma** | The fixed number of verses a stotra is chanted in, produced by repeating its three verses in set patterns: *trivṛt* (9), *pañcadaśa* (15), *saptadaśa* (17), *ekaviṃśa* (21) and higher | Pañcaviṃśa Brāhmaṇa; named in Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.3.4 |
| **Stobha** | Meaningless sung syllables (hāu, hoi, etc.) inserted into a sāman | Aitareya Āraṇyaka 2.3.4 and the Sāmavedic tradition |
| **Udgītha** | The central portion of a sāman, sung by the udgātṛ; the Chāndogya's great meditation symbol, identified with Om | Chāndogya Upaniṣad 1.1 |
| **Nivid** | Short invocations naming a deity's attributes, inserted into certain śastras | Aitareya Brāhmaṇa; preserved among the Ṛgveda khilas |
| **Āprī** | A class of Ṛgveda hymns recited in the animal sacrifice, each verse inviting one of the Āprī deities; the first is Ṛgveda 1.13 | Ṛgveda; Nirukta 8; Bṛhaddevatā |
| **Dānastuti** | "Praise of a gift": verses at the end of some Ṛgveda hymns thanking a patron | the commentarial and Anukramaṇī tradition |

When later centuries call any devotional poem a "stotra", they are borrowing the Vedic word but not its meaning. The sung-versus-recited distinction disappears; the Amarakośa already lists stotra and stuti as synonyms.

## Later forms named by number

Once the stotra became a free-standing devotional poem, the commonest way to name it was simply **by count**. The number is part of the title; the classical source is the text in which the famous example is embedded, or its traditional author.

| Name | Count | Classic example | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Catuḥślokī** | 4 verses | Catuḥślokī Bhāgavatam | Bhāgavata Purāṇa 2.9.32–35 (2.9.33–36 in some editions): Viṣṇu's four-verse teaching to Brahmā, held to be the seed of the whole Purāṇa |
| **Pañcaka / Pañcaratna** | 5 ("five gems") | Gaṇeśa Pañcaratnam | attributed to Śaṅkarācārya |
| **Ṣaṭka / Ṣaṭpadī** | 6 | Viṣṇu Ṣaṭpadī | attributed to Śaṅkarācārya |
| **Aṣṭaka(m)** | 8 | Bilvāṣṭakam, Liṅgāṣṭakam, Madhurāṣṭakam (Vallabhācārya), Toṭakāṣṭakam (Toṭakācārya) | the most common devotional form; many anonymous |
| **Navaka** | 9 | Navagraha Stotra | attributed to Vyāsa |
| **Daśaka / Daśaślokī** | 10 | Daśaślokī | attributed to Śaṅkarācārya; the Nārāyaṇīyam of Nārāyaṇa Bhaṭṭattiri (1586) is organised in 100 daśakas |
| **Śataka** | 100 | Nīti-, Śṛṅgāra- and Vairāgya-śataka | Bhartṛhari; the form is kāvya as much as stotra |
| **Aṣṭottaraśata-nāmāvalī** | 108 names | for any deity | used in arcanā: one name, one flower |
| **Triśatī** | 300 names | Lalitā Triśatī | Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna |
| **Saptaśatī** | 700 verses | Durgā Saptaśatī (Devī Māhātmya) | Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, chapters 81–93 in the common numbering |
| **Sahasranāma** | 1,000 names | Viṣṇu Sahasranāma | Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parva 13.135 (critical edition; ch. 149 in the vulgate): Bhīṣma to Yudhiṣṭhira |
| | | Lalitā Sahasranāma | Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna: Hayagrīva to Agastya |

Two technical notes:

- **Nāmāvalī** ("row of names") is not a separate hymn but a *use* of one. The names of a sahasranāma or aṣṭottara are put into the dative with *namaḥ* ("oṃ viśvasmai namaḥ") so that a flower or akṣata can be offered with each.
- **Sahasra** often means "thousand" loosely. Commentators on the Viṣṇu Sahasranāma, beginning with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya, count the names carefully because some repeat; the title fixes the ideal, not always the arithmetic.

## Forms named by purpose

The second great naming system tells you **what the hymn does**. Most of these names take their authority from one famous example in the Itihāsa or Purāṇas, which later hymns imitate.

| Name | Literal meaning | What it does | Classic example and source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Kavaca** | armour | Places each part of the body under a named deity's protection, limb by limb | Nārāyaṇa Kavaca, Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.8 (taught to Indra through Viśvarūpa); Devī Kavaca, a Mārkaṇḍeya–Brahmā dialogue recited as a preliminary (aṅga) before the Saptaśatī — not part of the Devī Māhātmya chapters themselves |
| **Argalā, Kīlaka** | bolt, pin | Companion aṅgas that "unlock" and "secure" the Saptaśatī recitation | recited with the Devī Kavaca before the Saptaśatī |
| **Pañjara** | cage | A protective enclosure of names, like a kavaca | various Purāṇic and Tāntric pañjaras |
| **Hṛdaya** | heart | The essence of a deity, condensed for repetition | Āditya Hṛdayam, Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa (sarga 105 in the common text): Agastya teaches it to Rāma on the battlefield, 31 verses |
| **Dhyāna śloka** | meditation verse | Describes the deity's form so the worshipper can visualise it before the main hymn | the "yā kundendu…" verse for Sarasvatī; the "raktaṃ lambodaraṃ…" verse for Gaṇeśa in the Atharvaśīrṣa |
| **Suprabhātam** | auspicious dawn | Wakes the deity at daybreak | Veṅkaṭeśa Suprabhātam, attributed to Prativādi Bhayaṅkara Aṇṇan (15th century); its four parts are Suprabhātam, Stotram, Prapatti and Maṅgalāśāsanam |
| **Prātaḥsmaraṇa** | morning remembrance | Recalled on waking | Prātaḥsmaraṇa Stotra, attributed to Śaṅkarācārya |
| **Prapatti / Śaraṇāgati** | surrender | Formal self-surrender to the deity | the Prapatti section of the Veṅkaṭeśa Suprabhātam; Rāmānuja's Śaraṇāgati Gadya |
| **Karāvalamba** | support of the hand | A plea to be lifted out of saṃsāra | Lakṣmī-Nṛsiṃha Karāvalamba Stotra, attributed to Śaṅkarācārya |
| **Aparādha-kṣamāpaṇa** | asking pardon for faults | Closes worship by asking forgiveness for errors in it | Devī Aparādha-kṣamāpaṇa Stotra, attributed to Śaṅkarācārya |
| **Maṅgala / Maṅgalāśāsana** | benediction | Wishes auspiciousness on the deity; closes a recital | the Maṅgalāśāsanam that ends the Veṅkaṭeśa Suprabhātam |
| **Māhātmya** | greatness | A narrative glorifying a deity, place, vow or text | Devī Māhātmya, Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa; countless sthala-māhātmyas of temples |
| **Gītā** | song | A teaching given as dialogue in verse | Bhagavad Gītā, Mahābhārata, Bhīṣma Parva; Gaṇeśa Gītā (Gaṇeśa Purāṇa); Guru Gītā (attributed to the Skanda Purāṇa) |
| **Stavarāja** | king of hymns | A title of honour for a hymn held to be supreme | Gopāla Stavarāja |
| **Mālā-mantra** | garland mantra | A long prose mantra with many names and seed-syllables, strung like a garland | Hanumān Mālā-mantra and others in Tāntric manuals |

The common thread is that **the name states the hymn's job in a day of worship**: wake the deity (suprabhātam), see the deity (dhyāna), protect the worshipper (kavaca), praise (stotra), surrender (prapatti), ask pardon (kṣamāpaṇa), bless (maṅgala). Read in that order, these names trace a complete pūjā.

## Forms named by metre or style

A third set of names comes from **chandas**, the science of metre. Here the classical source is the prosody manual — Piṅgala's *Chandaḥsūtra*, the *Nāṭyaśāstra*'s metre chapters, and later handbooks such as the *Vṛttaratnākara* of Kedāra Bhaṭṭa.

| Name | What it means | Classical definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bhujaṅga / Bhujaṅgaprayāta** | "the serpent's gait": a hymn in that metre | 12 syllables per pāda, four ya-gaṇas (⏑ – – × 4), as in the Vṛttaratnākara | Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam, Śiva Bhujaṅgam (attributed to Śaṅkarācārya) |
| **Toṭaka** | a hymn in the toṭaka metre | 12 syllables per pāda, heavy on the 3rd, 6th, 9th and 12th syllables (⏑ ⏑ – × 4); described in Nāṭyaśāstra ch. 16 | Toṭakāṣṭakam, by Śaṅkara's disciple Toṭakācārya, who is named after it |
| **Daṇḍaka** | "the staff": a long unbroken run of rhythm | any metre of **more than 26 syllables per pāda**; Piṅgala, Chandaḥsūtra 7.33–36, and the Vṛttaratnākara | Śyāmalā Daṇḍakam, traditionally attributed to Kālidāsa |
| **Laharī** | "wave": a long, flowing poem of praise | a literary title, not a metre | Saundaryalaharī (attributed to Śaṅkarācārya, 100 verses); Gaṅgālaharī of Jagannātha Paṇḍitarāja (17th century) |
| **Gadya** | prose | prose kāvya, as opposed to verse (padya) | Rāmānuja's Gadyatraya: Śaraṇāgati, Śrīraṅga and Vaikuṇṭha Gadya |
| **Cūrṇikā** | a short rhythmic prose passage | prose closing a verse hymn, especially in Vaiṣṇava stotras | the cūrṇikās appended to many Śrīvaiṣṇava hymns |

Note what this tells us. A title such as *Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam* names the deity and the **metre** — not the number, not the purpose. A reciter in the tradition hears the title and already knows the rhythm in which to chant.

| Catuḥślokī to sahasranāma | devotional (by number) | named after their famous examples (Bhāgavata, Mahābhārata, Brahmāṇḍa, Mārkaṇḍeya) | genre convention |
| Kavaca, hṛdaya, gītā, māhātmya | devotional (by purpose) | Bhāgavata 6.8; Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa; Mahābhārata; Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa | genre convention with classical models |
| Suprabhātam, karāvalamba, kṣamāpaṇa, prātaḥsmaraṇa | devotional (by purpose) | medieval stotra literature (Śaṅkara attributions; Aṇṇan, 15th c.) | later |
| Bhujaṅga, toṭaka, daṇḍaka | devotional (by metre) | Piṅgala, Chandaḥsūtra; Nāṭyaśāstra 16; Vṛttaratnākara | classical prosody, later hymns |
| Laharī, gadya, cūrṇikā | devotional (by style) | literary titles; Rāmānuja, Jagannātha | later |

In one line: **sūkta and the ritual stotra belong to the Veda; stuti, stava and stotra as "any hymn" belong to the lexicon; everything else is named by number, purpose or metre**, and takes its authority from a famous example rather than from a definition.

A note of honesty: the attributions to Śaṅkarācārya and Kālidāsa above are traditional. Scholars doubt many of them, and the tradition itself often uses the great name as a mark of respect rather than a claim of authorship.
