Speech (*vāk*) unfolds in four stages, from the unmanifest **Parā** to spoken **Vaikharī**. The teaching comes in three layers. The Veda says speech has four quarters, three of them hidden. Bhartṛhari, the philosopher of grammar, names three levels: Paśyantī, Madhyamā and Vaikharī. The Tantric and Śaiva tradition adds **Parā** as their source. Together they describe a single path: desire → thought → word → sound.

## 1. Vedic roots

**Ṛgveda 1.164.45** (the Asya Vāmīya hymn of Dīrghatamas) is the foundational verse:

> *catvāri vāk parimitā padāni tāni vidur brāhmaṇā ye manīṣiṇaḥ |*\
> *guhā trīṇi nihitā neṅgayanti turīyaṃ vāco manuṣyā vadanti ||*

"Speech is measured in four quarters. The wise brāhmaṇas know them. Three lie hidden in the cave and do not stir; the fourth is what humans speak." The later tradition reads the three hidden quarters as Parā, Paśyantī and Madhyamā, and the spoken fourth as Vaikharī.

**How the early commentators read the four quarters.** The Nirukta (Pariśiṣṭa, around 13.9) records several readings:

- Oṃ and the three vyāhṛtis
- the grammarians' four word-classes: noun, verb, prefix and particle
- the speech of different kinds of beings

The "levels of consciousness" reading is a later one.

**Ṛgveda 10.71.4** (the Jñāna Sūkta): *uta tvaḥ paśyan na dadarśa vācam, uta tvaḥ śṛṇvan na śṛṇoty enām*. "One who looks does not see Speech; one who listens does not hear her." To the one she favours, Vāk reveals herself "as a loving wife to her husband." This is where the idea of *seeing* speech begins, the seed of the name **Paśyantī**.

**Ṛgveda 10.125** (the Vāk or Devī Sūkta): Vāk speaks as the goddess who carries the gods, enters all beings, and "breathes forth like the wind." She is the śakti of speech, later identified with Sarasvatī.

## 2. How speech is produced: Pāṇinīya Śikṣā (verses 6–7)

> *ātmā buddhyā sametyārthān mano yuṅkte vivakṣayā |*\
> *manaḥ kāyāgnim āhanti sa prerayati mārutam ||*\
> *mārutas tūrasi caran mandraṃ janayati svaram |*

"The Self grasps meanings through the intellect and stirs the mind with the desire to speak. The mind strikes the body's fire; the fire drives the breath; the breath, moving in the chest, produces sound." This is the physical side of the same journey:

1. **Ātmā**, the Self, grasps meaning through buddhi
2. **Manas**, the mind, is stirred by the wish to speak (vivakṣā)
3. **Kāyāgni**, the body's fire, is struck
4. **Māruta**, the breath, is driven upward
5. **Svara**, sound, arises in the chest

## 3. Bhartṛhari's three levels (Vākyapadīya, Brahmakāṇḍa)

Bhartṛhari (around the 5th century) treats language itself as Brahman (Śabda-Brahman). In Book 1 (verse ~1.142 or 1.159, depending on the edition) he calls grammar the path to "the highest station of the three-fold speech: Vaikharī, Madhyamā and Paśyantī."

- **Vaikharī** depends on breath. It is shaped by the places of articulation into separate, audible sounds and unfolds in sequence (*krama*).
- **Madhyamā** is inner, "middle" speech, held only in the intellect (*buddhi*). It still has sequence, but it is not sounded.
- **Paśyantī**, "the seeing one," has no sequence. Word and meaning are still one, like the yolk of a peahen's egg, which already holds every colour of the peacock before any appears. It is grasped as **pratibhā**, a flash of intuitive understanding. For Bhartṛhari this is the highest level, identical with Śabda-Brahman.

## 4. Parā added: Kashmir Śaivism and Śākta texts

- **Somānanda** (Śivadṛṣṭi, ch. 2) criticised the idea of Paśyantī as the ultimate.
- **Utpaladeva** and **Abhinavagupta** placed **Parā Vāk** above it (Parātrīśikā-vivaraṇa, Tantrāloka). Parā is Śiva's own self-aware consciousness (*vimarśa*), the "I" of the Supreme, in which the whole alphabet (*mātṛkā*) lies as pure potential.
- **Lalitā Sahasranāma** (names 366–371) names the Goddess as all four: *parā pratyakcitīrūpā paśyantī paradevatā | madhyamā vaikharīrūpā bhaktamānasahaṃsikā ||*. Bhāskararāya's commentary, the Saubhāgya-bhāskara, explains each name as one level of speech.
- **Śāradātilaka (ch. 1)** and **Yoga-kuṇḍalī Upaniṣad (ch. 3)** place the four levels in the body, rising with Kuṇḍalinī. The Upaniṣad puts Parā in the mūlādhāra, Paśyantī at the navel, Madhyamā in the heart and Vaikharī in the throat.

## 5. The four levels side by side

| | Parā | Paśyantī | Madhyamā | Vaikharī |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | supreme, beyond | "the seeing one" | "the middle one" | "the coarse" or "fully spread out" |
| **Nature** | unmanifest; pure consciousness and pure potential | word and meaning still one; seen, not spoken | thought-speech; words take form in the mind | audible, articulated speech |
| **Sequence (krama)** | none | none | sequence begins | full sequence of sounds |
| **Seat in the body** | mūlādhāra | navel (maṇipūra) | heart (anāhata) | throat and mouth |
| **Faculty** | the Self / Śiva | pratibhā (intuition) | buddhi and manas | prāṇa and the speech organs |
| **State of consciousness\*** | turīya | deep sleep | dream | waking |
| **Śakti\*** | — | icchā (will) | jñāna (knowledge) | kriyā (action) |
| **In Ṛgveda 1.164.45** | hidden | hidden | hidden | the fourth, spoken by humans |

\*These two rows are standard in later Tantric commentary; they are not in Bhartṛhari.

## 6. Why this matters for mantra and Jyotiṣa

A mantra works when it is carried back up this path: chanted aloud (*vaikharī*), then whispered and held in the mind (*madhyamā*), then seen (*paśyantī*), until it dissolves into its source (*parā*). That is why japa is taught to move from loud, to whispered (*upāṃśu*), to mental (*mānasa*).

The same sequence underlies the Jaimini teaching of **Bhavāt Bhāvam**: creation starts as desire, becomes thought, then word, then sound. Bhavāt Bhāvam reads that journey from seed to manifestation.
