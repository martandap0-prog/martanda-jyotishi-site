The **navāṃśa** (also *navamāṃśa*, D9) is the chart made by dividing every sign into **nine equal parts of 3°20′** and reading each part as a sign of its own. It is the most widely used of the sixteen divisional charts (*ṣoḍaśavarga*) taught by Parāśara, and in practice no birth chart is judged without it. The rule of thumb every student hears — *the rāśi shows the tree, the navāṃśa shows the fruit* — is not a verse from the classics, but it captures how the tradition uses it: the rāśi (D1) shows what is promised, and the navāṃśa shows how strongly and in what form that promise ripens.

This article explains how the navāṃśa is built, what Bṛhat Parāśara Horā Śāstra (BPHS) and Varāhamihira's Bṛhat Jātaka actually say about it, and what an astrologer reads through it. Where a point comes from later practice rather than a classical verse, it is marked as such.

## How the navāṃśa is made

Each sign of 30° is cut into nine parts of **3°20′**. Each part is assigned a sign by one counting rule, which Parāśara gives in a single verse:

> नवांशेशाश्चरे तस्मात्स्थिरे तन्नवमादितः । उभये तत्पंचमादेरिति चिन्त्यं विचक्षणैः ॥ *(BPHS 6.12)*
>
> *navāṃśeśāś care tasmāt sthire tannavamāditaḥ / ubhaye tatpañcamāder iti cintyaṃ vicakṣaṇaiḥ*

*"The navāṃśa lords: in a movable sign, from that sign itself; in a fixed sign, from its ninth; in a dual sign, from its fifth — so the wise should reckon."*

- **Movable signs** (Aries, Cancer, Libra, Capricorn): the first navāṃśa is the sign itself.
- **Fixed signs** (Taurus, Leo, Scorpio, Aquarius): the first navāṃśa is the 9th sign from it.
- **Dual signs** (Gemini, Virgo, Sagittarius, Pisces): the first navāṃśa is the 5th sign from it.

The nine parts then follow in zodiacal order. A simpler way to remember the same result: **fire signs begin from Aries, earth signs from Capricorn, air signs from Libra, water signs from Cancer.**

**The navāṃśa is the nakṣatra-pāda.** Each nakṣatra spans 13°20′ and has four pādas (quarters) of 3°20′. Twelve signs × 9 navāṃśas = 27 nakṣatras × 4 pādas = **108**. Counted from 0° Aries, the navāṃśas simply run Aries, Taurus, Gemini… round the zodiac nine times, so Aśvinī pāda 1 is Aries navāṃśa, pāda 2 Taurus, and so on. This is why a planet's navāṃśa is so sensitive to its exact degree, and why the navāṃśa ties the sign-based and the nakṣatra-based halves of Jyotiṣa together.

**A worked example.** A Moon at 14°10′ Taurus lies in the fifth part of the sign (13°20′–16°40′). Taurus is fixed, so counting starts from its 9th, Capricorn; the fifth part is Taurus. The Moon is therefore in Taurus in both charts — vargottama. In nakṣatra terms it is Rohiṇī pāda 2.

**The birth time matters.** The lagna moves through a whole navāṃśa in roughly 10–17 minutes, depending on the sign and latitude. A navāṃśa lagna is only as reliable as the recorded time of birth.

## What the classics say it signifies

**Parāśara assigns it to the spouse.** BPHS chapter 7, *Vargaviveka* ("discernment of the vargas"), opens by stating what each divisional chart is to be examined for. The navāṃśa line is short:

> नवमांशे कलत्राणां दशमांशे महत्फलम् । *(BPHS 7.3a)*
>
> *navamāṃśe kalatrāṇāṃ daśamāṃśe mahatphalam*

*"In the navāṃśa, [the matters] of the spouse; in the daśāṃśa, great results [of one's actions]."*

Set beside the other vargas, the pattern is clear: the lagna (D1) for the body, the horā (D2) for wealth, the dreṣkāṇa (D3) for siblings, the saptāṃśa (D7) for children, the daśāṃśa (D10) for great deeds (BPHS 7.1–3). Each varga is given one area of life, and the navāṃśa's is **kalatra** — the spouse and marriage.

**But it is also a measure of strength.** In the same chapter Parāśara groups the vargas into sets — six (*ṣaḍvarga*), seven (*saptavarga*), ten (*daśavarga*) and sixteen (*ṣoḍaśavarga*) — and gives each varga a share of twenty points (*viṃśopaka bala*) used to judge how strong a planet is overall. The navāṃśa appears in **every one of these sets**, and in the commonly used reading of the text it carries one of the largest shares: 5 of 20 in the ṣaḍvarga, and in the sixteen-fold scheme it ranks just below the rāśi and the ṣaṣṭyaṃśa (D60). This is the classical basis for treating the navāṃśa as the second chart after the rāśi: it is not only a spouse chart, it is part of how *every* planet's strength is weighed.

**Varāhamihira on the navāṃśa.** The Bṛhat Jātaka names the navāṃśa in its first chapter and singles out one class of navāṃśa as auspicious — the vargottama (next section).

**What is later teaching.** Two widely repeated ideas are interpretive rather than direct quotations:

- *The navāṃśa shows the "fruit" or the second half of life.* This is a teaching tradition, not a BPHS verse.
- *The navāṃśa is the chart of dharma.* It is the ninth division, and the ninth house is dharma, so teachers link the two. This is an inference from the number nine, not a stated rule.

Both are useful, and both are faithful to how the classics *use* the navāṃśa. They are simply not the classics' own words.

## Why it must be seen

The rāśi chart places a planet in a sign; the navāṃśa tells you **what that planet is made of inside the sign**. Two planets in the same rāśi can sit in very different navāṃśas, and the classics treat that difference as real.

### Vargottama: the planet that stays in its own sign

When a planet (or the lagna) occupies the **same sign in the rāśi and the navāṃśa**, it is *vargottama*, "best in the varga". Varāhamihira defines it and calls it auspicious:

> वर्गोत्तमाश्चरगृहादिषु पूर्वमध्यपर्यन्ततः शुभफला नवभागसंज्ञाः । *(Bṛhat Jātaka 1.14a)*
>
> *vargottamāś caragṛhādiṣu pūrvamadhyaparyantataḥ śubhaphalā navabhāgasaṃjñāḥ*

*"In the movable, fixed and dual signs, the first, middle and last navāṃśas respectively are vargottama, and they give auspicious results."*

So the vargottama navāṃśa is the **1st** in a movable sign (0°–3°20′), the **5th** in a fixed sign (13°20′–16°40′) and the **9th** in a dual sign (26°40′–30°) — the bold cells in the table above. In practice a vargottama planet is read as **steady and dependable**: it behaves the same way on the surface and underneath, and it tends to deliver what the rāśi promises. A vargottama lagna is read the same way for the whole person.

### Dignity in the navāṃśa

The ordinary dignities — exaltation, own sign, friend's sign, enemy's sign, debilitation — are judged again in the navāṃśa. This is what the viṃśopaka scheme of BPHS chapter 7 formalises: a planet's strength is the sum of its standing across the vargas, and the navāṃśa is one of the heaviest components.

The practical consequences, as the tradition applies them:

- **A planet weak in the rāśi but strong in the navāṃśa** (for example debilitated in the rāśi, exalted or in its own sign in the navāṃśa) is read as improving with time and delivering more than it first seemed to.
- **A planet strong in the rāśi but debilitated in the navāṃśa** is read as promising more than it gives — the outer show is strong, the inner substance weak.
- **A planet in the same dignity in both** is confirmed: what you see in the rāśi is what you get.

These rules of thumb are standard teaching built on the viṃśopaka principle; they are not single verses.

### Puṣkara navāṃśa

Later manuals also name certain navāṃśas in each sign as *puṣkara* ("nourishing"), held to strengthen any planet placed in them. The doctrine does not appear in the BPHS verses quoted here, and the lists differ between authorities, so treat it as a secondary refinement.

## What you read through the navāṃśa

### 1. Marriage and the spouse

This is Parāśara's own assignment (*navāṃśe kalatrāṇām*), so it comes first. In the navāṃśa one looks at:

- the **7th house** of the navāṃśa and its lord;
- the navāṃśa placement of the **lord of the rāśi 7th house**;
- the condition of **Venus** (the kāraka of the wife, and of marriage generally) and, in many traditions, **Jupiter** for the husband;
- planets aspecting or occupying the navāṃśa 7th.

The rāśi chart says *whether and when* marriage is promised; the navāṃśa is read for the *quality* of the marriage and the nature of the spouse. A strong rāśi 7th with a weak navāṃśa 7th is read as a marriage that looks good but strains; the reverse as one that starts modestly and deepens.

### 2. The real strength of every planet

Because the navāṃśa is built into viṃśopaka strength, its most frequent everyday use is not marriage at all. Before predicting from any planet — a yoga-forming planet, a daśā lord, a house lord — the astrologer checks its navāṃśa: is it vargottama, exalted, debilitated, with friends or enemies? A rāja-yoga whose planets fall in debility in the navāṃśa is expected to give much less than one whose planets are well placed there.

### 3. Daśā results

When a planet's daśā runs, its results are coloured by where it sits in the navāṃśa as well as in the rāśi. Teachers often say the navāṃśa shows the *inner* experience of the period while the rāśi shows the outer events. This is a teaching heuristic rather than a verse.

### 4. Kārakāṃśa and the iṣṭa devatā (Jaimini)

In the Jaimini system, the sign occupied by the **ātmakāraka** (the planet with the highest degree in the chart) in the navāṃśa is the **kārakāṃśa** or *svāṃśa*. The Jaimini Upadeśa Sūtras (adhyāya 1, pāda 2) and the kārakāṃśa chapter of BPHS read a long series of results from it — the soul's inclinations, spiritual leanings, and, from the **12th from the kārakāṃśa**, the *iṣṭa devatā*, the deity that leads to liberation.

One caution: commentators differ on whether these kārakāṃśa results are to be read in the navāṃśa chart, in the rāśi chart counted from the navāṃśa sign, or both. Follow the method of your own paramparā consistently.

### 5. Dharma and the inner person

Read in the tradition as the "chart of dharma", the navāṃśa is used to judge a person's inner disposition, values and spiritual capacity — the strength of the navāṃśa lagna and its lord, the 9th of the navāṃśa, and the condition of Jupiter. As noted above, this is a teaching inference from the number nine and from the kārakāṃśa doctrine, not a separate BPHS rule.

## Reading it in practice

A simple order of work for any chart:

1. **Check the birth time.** If the navāṃśa lagna falls within a few minutes of a navāṃśa boundary, hold conclusions drawn from the navāṃśa lagna and its houses lightly. Planetary navāṃśas (except the Moon's) are far more stable than the lagna's.
2. **Mark the vargottama points.** Note every planet, and the lagna, that is vargottama. These are the most reliable parts of the chart.
3. **Judge each planet's dignity in both charts.** Make a two-column list — rāśi dignity, navāṃśa dignity — for the lagna lord, the Moon, the yoga-forming planets and the running daśā lords.
4. **Read marriage from the navāṃśa 7th**, its lord, Venus and Jupiter, together with the rāśi 7th lord's navāṃśa.
5. **Read the kārakāṃśa** if you work in the Jaimini system: the ātmakāraka's navāṃśa sign, and the 12th from it.
6. **Only then synthesise with the rāśi.** The rāśi remains the primary chart. The navāṃśa refines, confirms or qualifies what the rāśi promises; it does not replace it.

### Common mistakes

- **Reading the navāṃśa as a separate horoscope.** Its houses are meaningful, but it is a division of the rāśi, not an independent chart. Every navāṃśa judgement should be tied back to the rāśi.
- **Trusting the navāṃśa lagna on a doubtful birth time.** A difference of ten minutes can change it.
- **Treating the navāṃśa as "the marriage chart" only.** Parāśara does assign it to the spouse, but the same chapter makes it a pillar of every planet's strength.
- **Over-reading one placement.** A single debilitated planet in the navāṃśa does not cancel a strong rāśi. Weigh it as one input among the vargas, as the viṃśopaka scheme intends.
